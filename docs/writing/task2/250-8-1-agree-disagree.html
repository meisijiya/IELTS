Task 2 — Domestic food production (agree-disagree)

Some people suggest that a country should try to produce all the food for its population and import as little food as possible. To what extent do you agree or disagree? (2022.12.31)

立场: 不完全同意，应以国内基本口粮为主并允许有限进口; 理由1: 国内生产保障粮食安全与农村生计; 理由2: 有限进口补充气候短板并稳定价格

Some commentators argue that every nation should grow all of its own food and reduce imports to a bare minimum, in order to strengthen food security and protect farmers. While I appreciate the appeal of self-sufficiency, I only partly agree, because total self-reliance is rarely feasible and a carefully balanced mix of domestic production and limited trade serves citizens better.

There is a compelling argument for producing staple foods at home. When a country grows its own rice, wheat and vegetables, it is far less exposed to sudden currency shocks, shipping disruptions or political pressure from foreign suppliers, and its farmers enjoy stable demand that keeps rural communities alive. A clear example is the long-standing policy in Japan of supporting domestic rice farming, which has preserved countryside villages and shielded consumers from the price spikes that followed the 2008 global food crisis.

However, insisting on producing everything can be both wasteful and dangerous. Some climates cannot grow coffee, cocoa or certain grains economically, so citizens would lose access to nutritious and culturally important foods if imports were banned. Heavy reliance on a single domestic harvest also leaves a country vulnerable to drought, flood or pest outbreaks that can wipe out an entire season's supply. Limited imports from a diverse set of trading partners spread the risk and keep prices stable, complementing rather than undermining local agriculture.

In conclusion, I partially agree that countries should produce their core food at home, but I still believe that a small, well-managed level of import is necessary for nutrition, climate resilience and price stability, so the wisest policy is balance rather than full self-sufficiency.
