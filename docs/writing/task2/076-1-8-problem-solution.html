Task 2 — Fewer science students (problem-solution)

Nowadays, not enough students choose science subjects in university in many countries. What are the reasons for this problem? What are the effects on society? (2024.3.16)

立场: 问题严重，需要重视; 理由1: 理科课程难度高、就业压力大、收入预期不如商科，导致学生回避; 理由2: 社会层面将面临科研人才短缺、技术创新放缓，长期影响竞争力。

The aim of this essay is to analyse the main reasons why too few university students opt for science subjects in many countries, and to consider the consequences this trend may have for society.

Several factors help to explain the declining appeal of science degrees. First, these programmes are widely perceived as intellectually demanding and time-consuming, requiring long hours in laboratories and a strong foundation in mathematics. Many prospective students, fearing poor grades and endless coursework, choose softer alternatives such as business or media studies. Second, graduates in science and engineering often face uncertain job prospects in the short term, while their peers in commerce or law can secure higher salaries much more quickly after graduation. When families are investing heavily in tuition fees, the perceived return on investment heavily influences the choice of major.

The effects of this trend on society are serious and far-reaching. A persistent shortage of scientists and engineers slows down innovation in fields such as medicine, renewable energy and artificial intelligence, on which long-term economic growth depends. Industries that rely on advanced research may relocate to countries with stronger talent pipelines, reducing domestic employment. Beyond economics, a population with limited scientific literacy is also more vulnerable to misinformation on issues ranging from public health to climate change, eroding the quality of public debate.

In conclusion, the under-attractiveness of science degrees stems from academic difficulty and unfavourable career comparisons, and the resulting talent shortage poses a serious threat to innovation and public reasoning. Governments and universities should therefore address this problem by improving career prospects for scientists and promoting scientific literacy from an early age in schools.
