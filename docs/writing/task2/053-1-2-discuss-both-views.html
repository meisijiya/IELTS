Task 2 — Children freedom to make mistakes (discuss-both-views)

Some people believe that children have the freedom to make mistakes, while others argue that adults should prevent them from doing so. Discuss both views and give your own opinion. (2021.8.7)

立场: 倾向允许犯错，但对不可逆后果设红线; 理由1: 试错是学习的必经过程; 理由2: 某些错误后果超出孩子承受能力

There is ongoing debate about whether children should be allowed the freedom to make mistakes or whether adults should step in to prevent them. Some argue that mistakes are essential to learning, while others insist that responsible adults must shield children from harm and regret.

Those who support giving children the freedom to err emphasise that trial and error is the most natural way humans learn. When a child drops a cup, loses a match or chooses the wrong friends, the disappointment that follows leaves a deeper imprint than any lecture could. These setbacks build a growth mindset: the belief that ability improves through effort and reflection rather than talent alone. A musician who has practised through imperfect performances usually outperforms a peer who has been shielded from every awkward note.

Advocates of adult intervention, however, warn that some mistakes carry consequences that children are not yet mature enough to absorb. Decisions about drugs, online privacy or reckless driving can leave scars that last a lifetime, and parents who fail to set firm boundaries may discover that freedom without guidance is little more than neglect. In a world that exposes young people to pressures that previous generations never faced, active adult guidance has become more necessary than ever.

In my view, both arguments have merit, and the answer lies in distinguishing between reversible mistakes and irreversible ones. Adults should let children stumble over reversible choices, while remaining firm about the few decisions whose consequences cannot be undone.

In conclusion, I am more inclined to support the principle of allowing children to make mistakes, provided that adults draw clear lines around irreversible harm, because guided freedom produces wiser, more resilient adults than either pure permissiveness or excessive control.
