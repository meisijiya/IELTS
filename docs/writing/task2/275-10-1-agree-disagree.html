Task 2 — Young offenders (agree-disagree)

Young people who commit crimes should be treated in the same way as adults. To what extent do you agree or disagree? (2023.8.12)

立场: 不同意少年犯与成年犯同等对待; 理由1: 青少年大脑尚未发育成熟,改造空间大于成年人; 理由2: 关进成人监狱会加剧污名化并提高再犯率

Some people argue that young people who commit serious crimes should be punished in exactly the same way as adult offenders. I firmly disagree with this view, because adolescents are still developing both mentally and emotionally, and treating them as fully formed criminals would damage their future and, paradoxically, increase rather than reduce future offending.

The first reason is that the adolescent brain is still maturing, which means young offenders remain highly responsive to education and therapy. Neurological research consistently shows that the parts of the brain responsible for impulse control are not fully developed until the mid-twenties. Were teenagers to be locked away in adult facilities, they would lose precisely the years in which targeted intervention could redirect their behaviour. A clear example can be seen in juvenile justice systems across Northern Europe, where restorative programmes have measurably lowered reoffending rates among teenagers compared with punitive adult prisons.

The second reason is that treating minors as adults deepens the stigma that drives them towards further crime. When a sixteen-year-old is housed alongside hardened adult criminals, they tend to absorb more harmful habits rather than learn restraint, and on release they are marked for life as dangerous outcasts. Family ties weaken during long sentences, education is interrupted, and the legitimate job market becomes almost impossible to enter. Were society to apply the harshest penalties to young offenders regardless of age, it would effectively write off an entire generation before they could contribute. Investment in age-appropriate rehabilitation, by contrast, protects both the individuals concerned and the communities they will eventually rejoin.

In conclusion, I still believe that young offenders deserve responses calibrated to their age, because punishment shaped for adults ignores both their developmental needs and their potential to reform.
