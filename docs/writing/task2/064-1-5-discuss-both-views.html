Task 2 — Evaluating teachers (discuss-both-views)

Many schools encourage students to evaluate and criticize their teachers in order to improve the quality of education, while others say this will lead to the loss of respect of teachers. Discuss both views and give your own opinion. (2021.4.29)

立场: 建设性反馈利大于弊,但需以尊重为前提; 理由1: 反馈帮助教师反思与改进教学质量; 理由2: 失当批评会侵蚀教师权威与课堂秩序

Whether students should be encouraged to evaluate and even criticise their teachers is a question that has divided school boards, parents and educators. Some maintain that such feedback is essential for improving educational quality, while others fear that it will erode the traditional respect owed to teachers. Each side raises concerns that deserve careful consideration.

Supporters of student evaluation argue that teachers, like other professionals, need honest feedback in order to refine their craft. A clear example is the way anonymous end-of-term surveys help lecturers notice that their explanations have become too rapid or their examples too abstract, problems that colleagues rarely flag and that students are best placed to observe. Were such feedback channels to be closed, many mediocre instructors would continue using methods that disengage their classes, simply because no one is willing to tell them that their teaching has deteriorated.

Critics, however, point out that inviting open criticism from adolescents, who are still developing emotional regulation, risks turning classrooms into arenas of confrontation rather than learning. In schools where student evaluations are conducted carelessly, teachers sometimes report being publicly shamed over minor issues, which can damage morale and discourage talented graduates from entering the profession. Furthermore, if young people are taught that questioning authority is the norm, the general culture of respect that holds a school together may gradually erode.

Having considered both perspectives, I am more inclined to support structured, anonymous feedback that allows students to comment on teaching quality while protecting teachers from personal attack. In my view, this balanced approach preserves the authority of the profession and still gives educators the honest signals they need to improve.
