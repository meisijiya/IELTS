Task 2 — International news in schools (discuss-both-views)

Some people think that secondary school students should study international news as one of their subjects, but others believe that it is a waste of their valuable study time. Discuss both views and give your opinion. (2023.6.24)

立场: 倾向支持，但应整合进现有学科; 理由1: 国际新闻培养全球素养与批判阅读; 理由2: 国际新闻若单独设科会挤占核心课程时间

There is ongoing debate about whether secondary school students should study international news as a formal subject. Some argue that global current affairs deserve a dedicated place in the timetable, while others believe this would consume time better spent on traditional academic subjects.

Those who support adding international news to the curriculum emphasise that young people today grow up in a deeply interconnected world. Studying international news helps teenagers develop what educators call global literacy: the ability to read beyond headline slogans, weigh competing sources and understand how politics, economics and culture interact across borders. A clear example is the way a class discussion of climate negotiations can sharpen students' reasoning about evidence, fairness and long-term thinking, skills that any well-rounded citizen needs.

Opponents, however, warn that adding a new subject risks overloading an already crowded timetable. Core disciplines such as mathematics, science and language are widely seen as the foundation of academic success and future employment, and many parents worry that time spent reading the news will come at the expense of these more measurable outcomes. Schools therefore face a difficult choice between breadth and depth, especially in the final years before university entrance examinations.

In my view, both concerns can be reconciled. International news need not be taught as a stand-alone subject; it can be woven into existing lessons such as history, geography and English, where it reinforces critical reading without claiming extra hours. Such integration keeps academic rigour intact while still cultivating the global awareness that young citizens need.

In conclusion, I am more inclined to support the inclusion of international news in secondary education, because its benefits for global literacy outweigh the risks of timetable congestion, provided it is integrated thoughtfully.
