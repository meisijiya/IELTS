Task 2 — Money management for graduates (problem-solution)

A lot of young people do not know how to manage their money when graduating from high school. What do you think are the reasons? What can be done to help them improve this important skill?

立场: 揭示教育与家庭两重缺口，并提出系统化补救方案; 理由1: 学校缺乏财商课程+家庭避谈金钱; 理由2: 高中阶段必修财商+家庭预算实践

The aim of this essay is to examine why so many young people leave high school without basic money management skills and to suggest practical ways to help them acquire these abilities.

Several reasons explain this widespread gap. To begin with, most secondary school curricula concentrate on academic subjects and ignore personal finance altogether, so graduates often meet their first payslip with no idea how to budget, save or avoid debt. Furthermore, many families treat money as a private topic and never involve their children in decisions about rent, groceries or monthly bills. As a result, teenagers absorb the cultural message that financial matters are something to worry about only when problems appear, rather than a routine skill to be learned. The easy availability of consumer credit and digital payment apps then makes the problem worse, because young adults can spend invisibly and overspend without noticing until bills pile up.

The most effective solution would combine school education with family practice. High schools should make a compulsory personal finance course part of graduation requirements, covering topics such as budgeting, compound interest, credit-card traps and basic investing, ideally through simulations rather than lectures. At the same time, parents can invite older teenagers to share responsibility for household budgeting, allowing them to plan grocery lists, compare utility tariffs and track monthly subscriptions. Were banks and community organisations to offer free short workshops for school leavers, the transition into independent financial life would become far smoother.

In conclusion, the reasons for poor money management among graduates lie in education and family habits, and a combination of school courses, parental involvement and community workshops offers the most promising path forward.
