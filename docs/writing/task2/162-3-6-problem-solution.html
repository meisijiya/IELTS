Task 2 — Unsuitable university courses (problem-solution)

Some college freshmen find that the courses they choose are not suitable for them. What are the causes of this? What can be done to solve the problem? (2022.9.3)

立场: 揭示高中与大学课程衔接不足，并提出入学引导与试读制度方案; 理由1: 高中生缺乏职业信息与课程内容了解; 理由2: 大学入学指导与第一年试读可减少错误选择

The aim of this essay is to examine why a growing number of college freshmen discover that the courses they have selected do not suit their interests or abilities, and to suggest practical measures that universities and secondary schools could adopt to reduce this disappointment.

Two main causes explain the mismatch. The first is the limited information available before students apply. High schools rarely provide detailed comparisons between similar majors, and parents often urge children towards prestigious-sounding subjects rather than those that fit their temperament. Many applicants choose a course because of a vague family expectation or an inspiring teacher, only to discover in their first semester that workload and career outcomes differ from what they had imagined. The second cause is the pressure to commit early. University systems in many countries require applicants to nominate a specific major months before graduation, and changing course after enrolment can be bureaucratically painful, so students feel locked in.

Several measures could ease the problem. Secondary schools should arrange regular meetings with university lecturers, alumni and industry professionals so students gain realistic pictures of different careers before they apply. Universities could delay specialisation by adopting a broad first-year curriculum that lets students sample subjects before declaring a major, and could streamline the process of switching courses without losing credits. Mentorship programmes pairing freshmen with upper-year peers would also help them identify early whether their chosen field matches their strengths, and would reduce the isolation that drives dissatisfied learners to drop out.

In conclusion, unsuitable course choices stem from poor information and premature specialisation, and the solution lies in earlier career guidance at school, broader first-year programmes at university and easier pathways for students who recognise that a different direction would suit them better.
