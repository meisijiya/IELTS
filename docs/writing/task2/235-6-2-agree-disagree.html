Task 2 — Advertising unnecessary products (agree-disagree)

Some people say that the main aim of advertising is to improve the sales of products that people do not really need. To what extent do you agree or disagree? (2021.7.17)

立场: 部分同意（商业广告主要目的是创造需求，但信息广告仍有价值）; 理由1: 商业广告以情感与身份认同驱动消费，制造伪需求; 理由2: 信息类广告帮助消费者了解真实有用的新产品

Some commentators argue that the principal purpose of advertising is to push sales of products that consumers do not genuinely need. I largely agree with this view of commercial campaigns, while recognising that a smaller, informative kind of advertising still performs a useful role in well-functioning markets.

The first reason is that most modern advertising works by appealing to emotion, status and identity rather than to the practical value of what is being sold. A soft drink, a luxury handbag or a new smartphone model is rarely essential in any objective sense, yet glossy campaigns persuade millions to upgrade what they already own. The techniques used are remarkably sophisticated: marketing departments hire psychologists, study social media behaviour and tailor messages to personal insecurities so that viewers feel they are buying a better version of themselves rather than a simple object. From this perspective, the central aim is to manufacture desire where genuine need does not yet exist.

The second reason is that a more informative kind of advertising still plays a legitimate role. When a new energy-saving appliance reaches the market, clear advertising helps households learn about cost savings and environmental benefits they would otherwise miss. Public-health campaigns on vaccination or safe driving similarly use the same channels to encourage behaviour that genuinely improves well-being. These examples show that advertising is not always about selling unnecessary goods, and they justify stricter rules that distinguish informational messages from the manipulative campaigns that dominate commercial media.

In conclusion, I still believe that the main aim of most commercial advertising is indeed to boost sales of products people do not really need, though the practice can serve society better when it is limited to honest information about genuinely useful goods and services.
