Task 2 — Teachers teach morality vs academic (discuss-both-views)

Some people believe teachers should teach students to judge what is right or wrong and to behave well. Others say teachers should only teach students academic subjects. Discuss both views and give your opinion. (2025.4.26)

立场: 倾向教师兼顾学术与品格教育; 理由1: 学校是品格教育的天然场域; 理由2: 把道德评估强加给教师会稀释学术本职

Whether teachers should focus exclusively on academic subjects or also guide students in matters of right and wrong has long divided parents and educators. Some argue that moral education belongs to the family, while others believe that schools have a responsibility to shape young people's character as well as their knowledge.

Those who support a broader role for teachers point out that schools are places where children from every background regularly meet under adult supervision. In practice, many pupils spend more waking hours with their teachers than with their parents, and lessons are full of moral moments that no syllabus formally addresses: how to share fairly in a group project, how to admit a mistake, or how to disagree without resorting to insult. A clear example is the way a teacher who calmly resolves a playground dispute teaches conflict resolution more powerfully than any textbook can.

Advocates of an academic-only role, however, warn that overloading teachers with moral duties risks diluting their core mission. They argue that subject specialists are trained to deliver measurable learning outcomes, not to act as surrogate parents, and that expecting them to evaluate students' character invites confusion and unfairness. In a system already crowded with examinations, additional pastoral work may simply spread teachers too thin and damage academic standards.

In my view, both positions have merit, and the best solution is for teachers to weave character development into academic lessons rather than treat it as a separate duty. When a history lesson discusses justice, teachers can cultivate integrity without adding hours to the timetable.

In conclusion, I am more inclined to support a dual role for teachers, because academic learning itself offers the ideal vehicle for such guidance.
