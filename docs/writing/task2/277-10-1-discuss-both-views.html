Task 2 — Crime origins (discuss-both-views)

Some people think most crime is the result of circumstances, e.g. poverty and other social problems. Others believe that most crime is caused by people who are bad by nature. Discuss both views and give your own opinion. (2024.4.13)

立场: 大多数犯罪是社会环境的产物而非天性; 理由1: 贫困、家庭破裂等结构性因素与犯罪率高度相关; 理由2: 天生犯罪人的生物学证据薄弱且无法解释地区性集中

It is sometimes claimed that most crime is caused by people who are simply bad by nature, while others argue that social circumstances such as poverty drive people to offend. This essay discusses both views before giving my own opinion.

Those who emphasise personal nature point to the existence of repeat offenders whose crimes appear unrelated to upbringing or income. Some research does suggest that certain individuals possess personality traits, such as low empathy or impulsivity, that make criminal behaviour more likely regardless of environment. From this perspective, prisons exist primarily to restrain such people from harming the rest of society, and rehabilitation is unlikely to succeed with those who are fundamentally disposed towards wrongdoing.

On the other side, advocates of the social-causation view stress that crime rates rise and fall with economic conditions. When jobs disappear, families fragment, and decent housing becomes unaffordable, ordinary people face pressures that some eventually resolve through illegal means. They point to neighbourhoods where almost every young man has been in trouble with the police and ask whether so many people in one place could really all be born bad. International comparisons also show that societies with strong welfare systems and well-funded schools tend to record noticeably lower crime rates.

My own view is that the social-causation explanation is far more convincing for the overwhelming majority of crimes. While a small number of offenders may indeed have stable antisocial tendencies, policy built around that idea would neglect the vast majority whose crimes are rooted in deprivation, exclusion, and lack of opportunity.

In conclusion, societies seeking to reduce crime should invest in education, employment and community support, rather than assuming that offenders are simply wicked by nature.
