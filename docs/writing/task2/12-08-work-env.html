Task 2 — Workplace dress code (agree-disagree)

Some people believe that employers should not be concerned about the way their employees dress but be concerned about the quality of work. To what extent do you agree or disagree? (2021.10.23)

It is sometimes argued that employers should not care about how their employees dress, since what really matters is the quality of their work. While I accept that output is the most important measure of a job well done, I disagree with the suggestion that appearance is irrelevant, because the way staff present themselves can directly affect colleagues and customers in many workplaces.

Those who dismiss dress codes point out that talented workers should be judged by results, not by what they wear. In creative industries such as software design or advertising, employees often produce their best ideas when they feel relaxed, and a strict uniform policy may suppress this freedom. Moreover, focusing on clothing can create a hostile atmosphere when managers use it to criticise staff who already meet their targets, which can lower morale.

I believe, however, that how employees dress remains an important professional signal. First, in jobs that involve direct contact with clients — such as sales staff in a bank or doctors in a clinic — a neat appearance helps build the trust that customers rely on. A patient is more likely to feel confident in a doctor who wears a clean white coat than in one who arrives in a wrinkled T-shirt. Second, a reasonable dress standard also creates a sense of discipline among colleagues, reminding everyone that the workplace is a shared professional space rather than a private living room.

In conclusion, although the quality of work must always come first, I do not think employers should ignore the way their staff present themselves. A sensible dress policy supports the company's image and the morale of its team, which is why a moderate level of attention to appearance remains valuable.
