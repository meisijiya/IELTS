Task 2 — Ex-prisoner reoffending (problem-solution)

Some ex-prisoners commit crimes after being released from the prison. What do you think is the cause? How can it be solved? (2023.1.14)

立场: 重新犯罪根因是就业障碍与社会污名; 措施1: 服刑期间提供职业培训并给雇主税收激励; 措施2: 出狱后提供过渡性住房与社区帮扶

It is widely observed that a troubling number of ex-prisoners commit crimes again shortly after being released. This essay aims to examine the underlying causes of this phenomenon and to propose realistic measures that governments and communities can take to address it.

The most significant cause of reoffending is the absence of stable employment combined with the social stigma that follows former inmates for life. Many employers are reluctant to hire anyone with a criminal record, which forces ex-prisoners into informal work that pays poorly and offers no security. Without a legitimate income, basic needs such as rent and food become difficult to meet, and the temptation to return to old criminal networks grows stronger by the day. The lingering stigma further isolates former offenders from supportive relationships, leaving them with little incentive to remain law-abiding once they leave the prison gates.

The most effective solution would combine vocational training during incarceration with active employer engagement after release. Prison workshops that teach practical skills, ranging from coding to welding, give inmates a concrete way to earn an honest living once they return to society. Tax incentives offered to companies that hire former offenders can reduce the recruitment bias that currently blocks so many capable workers. Equally important are halfway houses and mentoring programmes, which provide the stable housing and emotional support that families often fail to offer. When ex-prisoners are surrounded by people who expect them to succeed, they are far more likely to make the most of the second chance they have been given.

In conclusion, reoffending can be substantially reduced if governments tackle its root causes through training, employer incentives, and structured aftercare support rather than relying on the threat of further punishment.
