Task 2 — Illiteracy in modern societies (two-questions)

Despite the increased access to education, a significant number of people cannot read or write. What are the disadvantages without these skills? And what actions should governments take? (2023.4.1)

立场: 阐述文盲在就业、健康与公民参与方面的多重劣势，并提出政府主导的扫盲与成人教育方案; 理由1: 文盲限制就业、健康决策与政治参与; 理由2: 政府需投入扫盲课程与社区数字培训

The aim of this essay is to examine the serious disadvantages faced by adults who cannot read or write in modern societies, and to consider what concrete actions governments should take to reduce the number of people still affected by illiteracy.

The disadvantages are wide-ranging. The most obvious is economic: employers increasingly expect written communication and basic computer use, so illiterate workers are confined to the lowest-paid, most insecure jobs and find it difficult to escape poverty even when their country as a whole is prospering. Illiteracy also damages health. People who cannot read medication labels, appointment letters or public health leaflets are more likely to miss warning signs, misuse prescribed drugs and miss childhood vaccinations. Equally serious is the impact on civic life. Without literacy, citizens struggle to understand ballot papers, contracts and official notices, which makes them easy prey for dishonest traders and sidelines them from political decisions that shape their communities.

Governments can take several effective actions. First, they should guarantee free, high-quality primary schooling for every child and enforce compulsory attendance, preventing future generations from joining the ranks of the illiterate. Second, ministries of education should fund adult literacy classes held in evenings and weekends at community centres, so working men and women can acquire reading skills without sacrificing income. Public libraries and mobile outreach teams could extend the network into rural villages. Third, governments should pair literacy programmes with practical content, including basic banking and digital navigation, so newly literate adults can immediately use their new skills in daily life.

In conclusion, illiteracy harms employment, health and civic participation in equal measure, and governments should respond with compulsory schooling for the young and accessible, practical adult classes for those who missed out first time around.
