Task 2 — Demand for instant services (two-questions)

Nowadays more and more people want to get things done instantly (services, information, tasks). Why is this? Do you think this is a positive or negative development? (2024.12.21)

立场: 揭示科技训练即时期待，并判断整体为负面发展; 理由1: 智能手机与算法喂养即时期待; 理由2: 负面为主因削弱耐心与深度思考

The aim of this essay is to explore why modern consumers have developed such a strong appetite for instant services, instant information and instant task completion, and to assess whether this cultural shift should be welcomed or treated with caution.

Two related factors explain the rise of instant gratification. The first is technological: smartphones, search engines and streaming platforms have trained users to expect a useful answer, a delivered meal or an entertainment stream within seconds, and people have gradually lost patience for anything that takes longer to arrive. The second factor is economic competition. App companies compete fiercely for attention, so they design algorithms that reward immediate response and penalise delay, reinforcing the feeling that waiting is a sign of poor service. Together these forces have reshaped ordinary expectations: anything that does not arrive within minutes is now treated as unreasonably slow.

I believe the trend is, on balance, a negative development. Convenience brings clear benefits, but the constant pressure for immediacy erodes patience, weakens the capacity for sustained attention and pushes people towards shallow decisions made in haste. When individuals demand instant answers to complex questions, they often receive simplified or misleading information, and when employers expect instant responses at all hours, employees suffer chronic stress and family life deteriorates. By contrast, traditional activities that demand waiting, such as cooking a slow meal, reading a long article or mastering a musical instrument, build skills and satisfaction that instant alternatives can never provide.

In conclusion, the hunger for instant results is fuelled by smart technology and competitive algorithms, and although speed brings genuine convenience, the deeper cost is a culture that loses patience, depth and the quiet rewards of deliberate effort.
