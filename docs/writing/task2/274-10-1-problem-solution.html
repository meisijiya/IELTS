Task 2 — Repeat offending (problem-solution)

Many offenders commit more crimes after serving the first punishment. Why is this happening, and what measures can be taken to tackle this problem? (2023.6.17)

立场: 重犯率高的根因是监狱重惩罚轻改造且出狱后缺乏支持; 措施1: 服刑期间开展教育与心理辅导; 措施2: 出狱后提供过渡性住房、就业与社区监督

Many offenders go on to commit more crimes after completing their first punishment, leaving societies less safe rather than more secure. This essay aims to analyse why this troubling pattern occurs and to suggest practical measures that governments can adopt to tackle the problem at its root.

The most important cause of repeat offending is that prisons in many countries still focus heavily on retribution rather than genuine rehabilitation. Inmates spend long sentences locked in cells, cut off from education and from any meaningful contact with the outside world, only to emerge with the same limited skills and damaged relationships they had on entry day. Compounding this, the social stigma attached to a criminal record closes most legitimate doors once they are released. Lacking work, housing, or supportive networks, even those who genuinely intend to reform often slide back into the only environment that still accepts them.

The most effective solution is to combine in-prison rehabilitation programmes with structured post-release support. Educational courses, vocational workshops and mental-health counselling delivered inside correctional facilities give prisoners the tools to build a different life after release. Equally crucial are halfway houses, supervised employment schemes, and mentors who follow former inmates through their first fragile months of freedom. When a released offender has somewhere stable to live, something meaningful to do each day, and at least one person who genuinely checks in on their progress, the lure of old habits gradually loses much of its hold. Governments that invest in such comprehensive aftercare ultimately save the far higher costs of repeated incarceration.

In conclusion, tackling repeat offending requires governments to treat prisoners as future citizens rather than as permanent outcasts, and to support them long after the cell door closes behind them.
