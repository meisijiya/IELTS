Task 2 — Consumer goods production (problem-solution)

The increase in the production of consumer goods results in damage to the natural environment. What are the causes of this? What can be done to solve this problem? (2022.8.13)

立场: 消费主义 + 短生命周期产品是主因, 需要立法、循环经济与公众教育共同应对; 理由1: 营销刺激下的过度消费、快时尚与一次性文化推高资源消耗与废弃物; 理由2: 解决方案包括生产者责任延伸、循环经济标准、可持续采购与消费者教育。

The aim of this essay is to explain why the rising production of consumer goods continues to damage the natural environment, and to discuss the measures that governments, producers and consumers can adopt to reduce that impact.

Several features of modern economies help to explain why consumer-goods production keeps growing at the planet's expense. Aggressive advertising, ever-shorter fashion cycles and the constant release of new smartphone models push households to replace perfectly functional items long before they wear out, sharply increasing the raw materials, energy and water needed for each replacement. Most products are also deliberately designed to be hard to repair, with glued batteries and proprietary parts that funnel customers back to the manufacturer. Globalised supply chains add further emissions by moving goods thousands of kilometres before they reach a shop.

A balanced response must combine stronger policy with industrial redesign and changes in shopping habits. Governments should enforce extended producer responsibility, requiring manufacturers to finance the collection and recycling of the items they sell, and to disclose their supply-chain emissions honestly. Design standards, such as the right-to-repair rules adopted in parts of Europe, can push producers towards modular, durable products. Companies can shift to circular models that refurbish, lease and recycle goods, while retailers can label items with credible environmental scores. Educational campaigns in schools can help people recognise that buying less, choosing durable goods and repairing what they already own is often the most powerful individual contribution.

In conclusion, the environmental damage caused by consumer-goods production stems from overconsumption and planned obsolescence, but regulation, better design and more thoughtful purchasing can place the global economy on a genuinely sustainable footing.
