Task 2 — Young vs adult education (agree-disagree)

The education of young people is highly prioritized in many countries. However, educating adults who cannot write or read is even more important and governments should spend more money on this. To what extent do you agree or disagree? （2021.7.10）

Many governments today give priority to the education of young people, but some argue that teaching adults who cannot read or write is more important and should receive more public money. While I accept that adult literacy deserves serious attention, I largely disagree with this view.

There are two main reasons why I hold this position. First, education for the young brings benefits that last for many decades. A child who learns to read and think clearly at school becomes an adult worker, parent and citizen who can support a family, follow medical advice and vote responsibly. The same investment, made at fifteen, keeps producing social returns for the next sixty years, while money spent on adult literacy usually helps only the learners themselves for a shorter period of working life. Second, young people are still forming their habits, so they learn faster and remember longer. A fifty-year-old taught to read will make progress, yet the effort needed is far greater than for a child of eight, and the new skill is more easily forgotten if not used every day.

I do accept that adult literacy programmes bring real social value. Helping older workers read safety notices, fill in forms or use digital services clearly improves their quality of life and reduces dependence on family members. Nevertheless, this practical benefit does not mean governments should shift large sums away from schools, since both groups need support and the long-term returns from educating children are simply larger.

In conclusion, I still believe that the education of young people should remain a higher priority than adult literacy programmes, although the latter clearly deserve attention and a reasonable share of the budget.
