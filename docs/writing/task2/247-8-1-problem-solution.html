Task 2 — Long-distance food trade (problem-solution)

Today food travels thousands of miles from the farm to the consumers. Why is this? Is it a positive or negative trend? (2021.7.31)

立场: 解释运输距离源自全球供应链与季节差，并判断为利弊并存需政策干预; 理由1: 全球化物流与季节互补推动长距离运输; 理由2: 负向明显但可通过本地采购与碳标签缓解

The aim of this essay is to explain why so much of the food eaten today travels thousands of miles before reaching the plate, and to consider whether this long-distance trade should be welcomed or treated as a cause for concern.

Two related forces explain the trend. The first is the rise of globalised supply chains. Large retailers now source strawberries from Chile in February, beef from Argentina and vegetables from Kenya, because international shipping has become cheap enough to make the journey profitable, and consumers expect the same produce all year round. The second factor is the contrast between seasons in the northern and southern hemispheres, which means a crop harvested in one region can be sold when the other is out of season, smoothing prices and giving shoppers genuine variety.

On balance, I regard the trend as a mixed one, leaning slightly negative unless action is taken. Long-haul food trade does widen choice and stabilise prices, but it also generates significant carbon emissions from refrigerated lorries and cargo planes, weakens local farmers who cannot match foreign costs and exposes distant markets to food-safety scandals that begin far away. These drawbacks can, however, be mitigated by clearer carbon labels for transported goods, by public procurement that favours local farmers, by shorter, more transparent regional supply chains that keep money inside the community, and by tougher safety inspections at every border crossing.

In conclusion, food travels far because global logistics and seasonal differences make it profitable, and although the practice brings real variety, only deliberate policy support for local farming and carbon labelling can prevent the trend from undermining both the climate and rural livelihoods.
