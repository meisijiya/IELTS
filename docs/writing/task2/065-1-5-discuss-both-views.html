Task 2 — Student evaluation of teachers (discuss-both-views)

Some people think in order to continually improve the quality of education, high school students should be encouraged to evaluate and criticize their teachers. Others feel that this would result in a loss of respect and discipline in classroom. Discuss both views and give your own opinion. (2022.1.20)

立场: 有限度的匿名评价优于公开批评; 理由1: 学生视角揭示教学盲区,推动改进; 理由2: 公开批评冲击纪律与教师尊严

Whether secondary-school students should be invited to evaluate and criticise their teachers is a question that provokes strong feelings on both sides of the debate. Some argue that this practice is essential for raising educational standards across the system, while others insist it will undermine discipline and erode the natural respect that teachers have traditionally commanded. Both arguments carry weight and merit close examination.

Advocates of student feedback observe that learners experience classroom instruction from a unique vantage point, one that even the most diligent head teacher cannot replicate. A clear example is the way a single teacher's monotone delivery may be flagged by students long before any official inspection notices the problem, prompting timely retraining that ultimately benefits the whole class. Without such grass-roots input, underperforming teachers can hide behind formal credentials for years, while their students quietly lose interest.

Opponents counter that adolescents are still maturing emotionally, and inviting them to publicly criticise adults in positions of authority can damage the classroom atmosphere that effective learning requires. In schools where pupils are encouraged to challenge teachers openly, lessons can quickly turn into debates over personality rather than subject matter, leaving little room for genuine instruction on the syllabus. Furthermore, the cumulative effect of constant criticism can demoralise veteran teachers and deter ambitious graduates from joining the profession at a time when many regions already face serious staffing shortages.

Having weighed both perspectives, I am more inclined to support anonymous, constructive evaluation rather than open criticism. In my view, well-designed feedback systems can help teachers improve their practice while still preserving the authority and dignity that the profession depends on to function effectively.
