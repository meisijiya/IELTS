Task 2 — Insufficient recycling (two-questions)

In some countries, there is not enough recycling of waste materials (e.g. paper, glass and cans). What are the reasons and solutions? (2023.8.5)

立场: 回收率不足由基建、习惯与监管滞后造成, 需要组合治理; 理由1: 垃圾分类不便、公众意识薄弱、企业动力不足导致可回收材料被填埋; 理由2: 引入押金返还、扩展分类基建、加强学校与社区教育、推动生产者责任可显著提高回收率。

The aim of this essay is to examine the main reasons why many countries still recycle only a small fraction of their paper, glass and cans, and to consider what practical solutions could raise these recycling rates to a more sustainable level.

Several explanations help to make sense of the persistently low recycling figures. The first is infrastructure: in many regions, household collections cover only a narrow range of materials, drop-off points are inconveniently located, and processing plants that handle mixed or contaminated waste are simply absent. The second is public behaviour: without clear signage and consistent reminders, busy households routinely toss recyclable items into general bins, particularly when shared bins raise uncertainty about what counts as recyclable. Finally, producers themselves have little incentive to design recyclable packaging, because the cost of disposal falls on local authorities rather than on the brands that create the waste.

Effective solutions must therefore act at every stage of the waste chain. Governments can expand kerbside collection, build modern sorting facilities and require clear, standardised labelling on all packaging. Deposit-return schemes for bottles and cans have already lifted recycling rates above ninety per cent in countries such as Germany and Norway, giving households a direct financial incentive to return empties. Extended producer responsibility can shift disposal costs back onto brand owners, encouraging them to redesign packaging with fewer, recyclable components. School programmes, community campaigns and easy-to-follow digital reminders can further turn recycling from an occasional effort into a daily social norm across entire neighbourhoods.

In conclusion, the shortage of recycling stems from a combination of weak infrastructure, inconsistent public behaviour and limited producer accountability, but coordinated investment, smart incentives and clearer rules together can transform wasted materials back into useful resources.
